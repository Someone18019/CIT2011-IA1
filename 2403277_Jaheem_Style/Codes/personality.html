AI as a Tool, and Not a Crutch

Artificial intelligence, in my experience, is best understood as a tool rather than a crutch. The real difference between the two lies in what access to knowledge actually means versus what intelligence means. Having access to information, through AI or any other resource, is not the same as truly understanding it. As one Stanford student put it in a university report on AI and critical thinking, 'You have to work to develop your writing skills, your personal voice, your opinions. For me, being smart is having access to knowledge, but being intelligence is knowing how to use that knowledge well. AI does not take away the thinking behind a task, it simply makes certain parts of that process faster, so long as I choose to engage with it that way.

I use AI primarily to expound on my own thoughts and ideas, to help explain key concepts I do not yet understand, and to make sure I grasp what I am actually doing rather than just copying an outcome. A clear example of this has been building this very website for my CIT2011 assignment. Since I joined this class late and started slightly behind, AI has helped me understand what functions and code elements do, how they work, and why they behave the way they do. If I do not know what something like an href attribute is for, I can ask and receive an explanation, then apply that understanding myself. In this way, AI has expanded my sources of knowledge and corrected me when I was wrong, without ever replacing my own effort.

I see the risk of AI becoming a crutch most clearly in the alternative approach, simply asking AI to generate a finished product without engaging with it at all. This connects to a point made in Stanford’s Center for Teaching and Learning guide for students, which quotes cognitive scientist Herbert Simon: "Learning results from what the student does and thinks and only from what the student does and thinks. The teacher can advance learning only by influencing what the student does to learn." I much prefer to physically type out code myself, because I am a practical thinker who learns by doing. If I instead relied on AI to generate entire functions for me without understanding them, I would gain nothing from the process. The real consequence would appear later, during an actual test or practical assessment, where I would be unable to apply knowledge I never truly received in the first place.

Lastly, AI has also been a great support to me in a different way: expression. I am not naturally a writer or someone who easily puts thoughts into words on paper. AI has given me a way to take ideas I already have and articulate them more clearly, helping me communicate what I think and know, rather than generating the thinking for me.

My Own Version of a Mentor

I never really had a traditional hero or mentor growing up, but I do have my own version of one, shaped by my own feelings, beliefs, and the things that genuinely bring me enjoyment. What guided me instead was my own sense of freedom, my own curiosity, and my own ability to focus deeply on the things that interested me. The only things that ever matched that description growing up were the army, and in my own experience, cadets. I spent my entire high school years in cadet as a dedicated NCO, and I thrived there because it gave me both a sense of freedom and the ability to do as I pleased, while also providing structure, order, responsibility, and accountability. That balance, freedom alongside discipline, has become my own personal definition of a mentor: a sense of duty, responsibility, accomplishment, curiosity, and genuine enjoyment, all at once.
